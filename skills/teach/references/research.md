# Research policy

Use source quality, relevance, and corroboration as the selection criteria. Popularity is a useful trust signal, but it never overrides primary evidence or subject authority.

## Source hierarchy

Prefer sources in this order:

1. Primary authority: official documentation, specifications, standards, original papers, maintainers, government material, and first-party technical publications.
2. Established institutions: universities, recognized foundations, professional bodies, major research organizations, and reputable public-interest organizations.
3. Recognized expert synthesis: material by established practitioners with directly relevant expertise and a visible body of work.
4. Established editorial publications: respected technical publishers and long-running publications with editorial review.
5. Community evidence: high-quality conference talks, repository discussions, forums, and widely referenced independent articles. Use these for intuition, practical experience, disputed behavior, or pitfalls, not as the sole basis for foundational claims.

Avoid content farms, copied documentation, unattributed summaries, affiliate-first comparisons, thin SEO pages, anonymous claims, and isolated personal posts without strong expertise or corroboration.

## Research process

1. Decompose the requested topic into the questions the guide must answer.
2. Locate the primary or official source for each core area.
3. Add independent sources where they improve explanation, establish real-world tradeoffs, or corroborate a consequential claim.
4. Check dates, applicable versions, author or publisher identity, and whether a source actually supports the intended claim.
5. Resolve disagreement by comparing scope, version, evidence, and authority. Surface meaningful uncertainty instead of manufacturing consensus.
6. Stop when each important area has adequate authoritative support; source count is not a quality metric.

Search ranking, social engagement, backlinks, citations, community references, and publisher reputation can indicate popularity. Do not claim a source is "popular," "most trusted," or "widely used" unless that claim itself is supported. Describe sources by verifiable authority instead.

For fast-changing technical topics, prefer currently maintained documentation and record the relevant product or specification version. Retain older sources only when they provide historical context that remains accurate.

## Citation rules

- Cite factual, version-sensitive, quantitative, safety-relevant, and contested claims near the text.
- Use compact numbered citation links such as `<a class="citation" href="#source-s1">[1]</a>`.
- Give each source a stable ID (`s1`, `s2`, and so on) and reuse the same ID for repeated citations.
- Link to the most specific canonical page, paper, or specification, not a search result or generic homepage.
- Never cite a page that was not inspected.
- Do not cite sources for the agent's transitions, analogies, or clearly labeled synthesis.

Each source ledger entry must include title, publisher or author, canonical HTTPS URL, source tier, publication or last-updated date when discoverable, access date, and a short reason for selection. Do not invent missing dates or popularity metrics; use `Not stated` where necessary.

When considering a source's image or illustration, separately verify its license or reuse policy. Permission to read or cite a page is not permission to copy its media. Capture the creator, original asset URL, license name and URL, and attribution requirements before embedding it. If any of those are unclear, use the source only for factual research and create an original visual instead.

Aim for multiple independent sources across the guide, not multiple citations attached mechanically to every sentence. One primary source can support a cluster of closely related claims. Consequential claims should be corroborated when suitable independent evidence exists.

## Sensitive topics

For medical, legal, financial, physical-safety, or other high-impact subjects, explain that the guide is general education, prioritize official and professional sources, distinguish established consensus from uncertainty, and avoid personalized professional advice.
